# Helix Compact

Backlink: [[../faction-balance]]

## Capsule

The Helix Compact is not a state in the conventional sense. It is a treaty-like consortium of port operators, insurers, cloud providers, satellite analytics firms, emergency logistics companies, and private security contractors that became too essential for states to command and too political to remain neutral.

## Historical Backstory

Helix emerged from the emergency privatizations of the Port Years. When governments could not move cargo fast enough, they granted long concessions to firms that could automate terminals, insure high-risk voyages, and finance stockpiles. The contracts were supposed to expire. Most were extended after each crisis because no ministry wanted to be blamed for a failed transition.

During the Glasshouse Buildout, Helix companies linked port automation, cargo telemetry, maritime insurance, satellite imagery, customs compliance, and financial authentication into one risk-pricing system. The system lowered costs in peacetime and quietly gave Helix a form of strategic command. It could not order a navy to sail, but it could make the voyage unaffordable, uninsured, unauthenticated, or invisible to customs.

The Helix Audit of 2023 revealed what many officers already suspected: modern mobilization had a private operating system. Helix executives insisted they provided resilience. Legislators saw capture. Veyr saw proof of hostile commercial encirclement. Sanctum saw outsiders pricing survival.

## Society And Politics

Helix personnel are not citizens of Helix, but the Compact has its own schools, arbitration courts, emergency badges, housing compounds, data enclaves, and casualty funds. Its officers speak the language of continuity, fiduciary duty, and infrastructure custodianship.

The Compact's legitimacy is contractual rather than patriotic. It can pay, protect, and predict. It struggles to ask people to die for it.

## Doctrine Culture

Helix favors:

- Control of movement permissions, insurance, data, and maintenance windows.
- Rapid cross-functional crisis cells.
- Commercial intelligence fused with satellite and network telemetry.
- Deniable contractors and security detachments.
- Legal arbitration as operational delay.
- Market pressure as a non-kinetic fires system.

It has superb visibility into logistics signatures but poor instinct for political sacrifice.

## S1-S5 Profile

| Function | Faction expression |
| --- | --- |
| S1 | Highly skilled specialists and contractors. Expensive to replace; loyalty depends on contracts, safety, and liability. |
| S2 | Exceptional commercial and technical visibility. Can over-trust data it owns and miss political intent. |
| S3 | Fast crisis cells and site security. Weak at sustained combat operations without state partners. |
| S4 | Best logistics picture in the setting. Vulnerable to labor action, legal seizure, and physical chokepoints. |
| S5 | Strong continuity planning and arbitration strategy. Weak public narrative when accused of governing without consent. |

## Gameplay Strengths

- Can shift external constraints such as shipping, electronics, insurance, and port throughput.
- Strong early warning from commercial signatures.
- Can create or remove sustainment bottlenecks.
- Flexible cells reduce stovepipe penalties.

## Gameplay Weaknesses

- Low public legitimacy under crisis.
- Contractor casualties and leaks create severe political backlash.
- Limited organic combat depth.
- States can nationalize assets if Helix overplays leverage.

## Signature Dilemmas

- Freeze risky traffic to protect infrastructure, or keep cargo moving to preserve legitimacy.
- Sell data to all sides as neutrality, or choose a side and become a target.
- Use private security to defend terminals, or invite state forces that may never leave.

## Internal Factions

- Continuity Directors want infrastructure kept running above all else.
- Risk Underwriters want premiums and exclusions to shape behavior.
- Security Principals want more armed authority at terminals and repair sites.
- Civic Trustees argue Helix must accept public oversight before it is broken up.

## Narrative Voice

Helix documents should sound technical, contractual, and quietly coercive. They rarely say "power"; they say "continuity authority," "risk exposure," and "service integrity."
