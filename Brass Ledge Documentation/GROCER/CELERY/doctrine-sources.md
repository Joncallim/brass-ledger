## Research Notes

FM 6-0 is the primary source for the staff logic used here. It describes S2 as the intelligence officer supporting current and future operations and plans, S3 as the principal operations officer for training, operations, plans, force development, synchronization, and orders, and S4 as the principal sustainment staff officer for logistics plans, supply, maintenance, transportation, services, and operational contract support.

The Marine Corps staff examples are useful for modern command-level framing: G-1 covers manpower and civilian personnel oversight, intelligence covers policy, planning, requirements, and readiness, G-3/5 fuses operations and plans, and G-4 covers logistics-related matters.

NATO's public command-structure material is useful for Brass Ledger's "supreme commander" framing because it emphasizes the relationship between political leaders, strategic commanders, military advice, and adaptive command structures.

The international doctrine pass adds a broader evidence base for later fictional faction design. NATO/UK/French/Japanese examples support a common J-structure baseline. Dutch doctrine adds the most explicit warning against pure vertical stovepipes. Australian doctrine adds a useful commander-centric planning model. Chinese official strategy adds a non-J-code pattern centered on active defense, system operations, civil-military integration, theater command, mobilization, and party control.

The tactical doctrine pass adds a small pattern library for objective, initiative, mass, economy of force, maneuver, surprise/deception, security, simplicity, tempo, culmination, reserve, and combined arms/interdependence. These patterns are for game design abstraction only; they should be expressed through staff debate and POTATO tradeoffs, not as real-world tactical instruction.

## Design Caution

Brass Ledger is not a doctrine simulator. CELERY uses doctrine to make the staff model legible, then POTATO converts it into playable abstraction.
